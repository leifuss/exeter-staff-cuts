Professorial pay — Russell Group comparators

Professors are paid on local, non-UCU scales, and how they climb them varies by university: some give automatic annual increments, others — including Exeter — advance only through a discretionary review, often every two years, and only some applications succeed. The model assumes, from national statistics, that the average professor is promoted to the lowest professorial grade at about age 48 and works to retirement at 67 (~9 biennial reviews) — individual careers will of course vary widely. You can tweak how likely each review is to grant a rise (the award rate), which is the main cost lever here; actual pay steps follow each university's local, non-UCU professorial scales — the national spine plays no role here. What differs between universities is the floor each ladder starts at (Sheffield £73.0k … Exeter £79.8k, York £80.4k), the size of each step (Southampton's rungs are ~1% apart, Exeter's ~3%), the number of rungs, and how often you climb (annual vs biennial) — and because progression is often slow, the starting floor does much of the work. Band 3 (rare, by exception) is excluded; weighted by Exeter's professoriate applied to every structure, and kept separate from the non-professorial models.

Read the per-head £ ranking below as indicative only — not a reliable cross-university comparison. Calibrated against Exeter's own accounts, the progression assumption this model relies on can place Exeter "below median" or "dearest" depending on a rate that a single year of accounts can't pin down (see the caveats, and the High pay tab). What the evidence does support: Exeter pays professors from a high floor (3rd of 14), on a slower documented cadence than most peers; it has the longest Band 2 (£89,746–£131,795 — a span of £42,049, against Sheffield's £31,485 next) and the highest Band 2 maximum of the twelve peers whose bands are directly comparable (Newcastle and Warwick are not banded in a comparable way); it has the most £100k+ staff per academic by headcount (upper-mid by FTE); and its recent £100k+ growth is mostly pay-award drift. The one concrete professorial-side saving is the voluntary 0.8 FTE lever tab. The model below is retained for exploration.

What the accounts corroborate

Exeter's 2024/25 Annual Report reports 404 staff earning £100,000 or more (basic pay, excluding employer pension) — with a striking bulge of 161 people in the £105,000–£110,000 band, up from 23 the year before. In 2024/25 that band sat exactly at the top rungs of Exeter's professorial ladder (£105,062 / £108,214) — on 2026-27 scales those rungs are £107,163 / £110,378, so the cohort has moved up a bin — consistent with a large cohort of long-serving professors parked near the ladder's upper end after years of biennial awards. Two implications: the model's professorial layer is pointing at a real, large population, and any structural saving on the professoriate would be concentrated among these ~400 highest-paid staff — the group best able to absorb slower progression, and the opposite end of the pay scale from the posts proposed for cuts.
