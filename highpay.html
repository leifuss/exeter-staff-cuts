High pay at Exeter — five years of £100,000+

Every annual report lists the number of staff paid £100,000 or more (basic pay, excluding employer pension contributions and the Vice-Chancellor, who is reported separately). This page collects that table from the last five published reports — six years of data, since each report also restates the previous year — and asks the obvious question: is the growth just pay awards pushing people over a fixed £100k line, or is something else going on?

Where they sit — distribution along the income axis

The £100k+ population for one year as a histogram on a true income axis: every column is one £5k band, all columns the same width but placed at their actual salary — so where bands are empty the tail spreads out to the right. Nearly all the mass sits at the bottom (£100–110k), where the £100k line cuts the professorial scale and the professoriate is densest; the 2024/25 spike of 161 in the £105–110k band is that cohort, lifted across by the 2024 award. The dashed green line is the award-adjusted (2019/20-equivalent) threshold for that year. The y-axis is fixed across years so the growth is visible; toggle headcount vs FTE (many of these are part-time — on an FTE basis the £105–110k spike is much smaller). ▲ marks Exeter's professorial pay points (rungs) at that year's rates: because staff can only sit on a rung and the rungs aren't evenly spaced, a £5k bin can contain one or two rungs — the £105–110k bin holds two (≈£105k and ≈£108k), which is a large part of why it towers over its neighbours. As awards nudge the rungs each year, which bin a rung falls in can shift, so some year-on-year movement is this bin/rung interaction rather than real change.

Is Exeter an outlier? Comparison with 13 Russell Group peers

The same £100,000+ disclosure from all 13 peers' 2024/25 accounts, normalised to £100k+ staff per 1,000 full-time academic staff (a single consistent denominator from HESA, not each provider's own varying staff categories). The reporting basis still differs — some publish headcount, some FTE (a part-time professor counts as less than one) — so those are kept in separate groups; Exeter is in both (it publishes both). The FTE group is the like-for-like comparison.
The honest answer: on headcount Exeter is a clear outlier (162 per 1,000, next is 126) — but that is inflated because its £100k+ staff are unusually part-time (0.63 FTE per head). On the fairer FTE basis Exeter sits upper-mid-pack, 4th of 9 — below Warwick, Leeds and Newcastle. So Exeter is high, but not the extreme outlier the raw headcount suggests.

What this means

Two things worth holding together. First, most of the headline tripling is arithmetic: the top of Exeter's professorial ladder sits just below £100k, so each national pay award pushes a large, pre-existing cohort across a fixed line — that is a fact about the threshold, not about hiring. Second, what remains after adjusting for awards is still real growth of roughly 30% in five years in the £100k+ (2019/20 terms) population — over the same period in which two voluntary severance rounds removed ~700 posts and ~150 more are proposed for cuts. The high-pay population grew while the workforce shrank.

What the distribution reveals about progression. Exeter's professorial scale runs SP54–SP71 (£78,189 to £129,211). The £100k+ population is densest just above the £100k line — a plateau of roughly 36–37 FTE on each of SP63, 64 and 65 (≈£102k/£105k/£108k), thinning higher, with a secondary Band-3 cluster around £120–125k. That plateau is diagnostic: about 179 FTE of professors sit in the £102–129k scale range. If professors really progressed by slow biennial merit (the assumption used on the Professorial tab), almost none would reach there in a career — the model predicts ~0%. The accounts say ~40% do. So Exeter professors in fact progress near-automatically to the top of Band 1; merit gating only bites crossing into Bands 2–3. This calibrates the professorial model: its slow-progression default is falsified here, and correcting it roughly doubles Exeter's modelled professorial premium (see the Professorial tab). The £5k bins then slice that plateau unevenly (the £105–110k bin catches two rungs — the ▲ markers), and part-time working inflates the headcount on top.

Is Exeter an outlier? The comparison above (all 13 English & Welsh Russell Group peers, per 1,000 full-time academic staff) gives a careful answer: on headcount, yes — Exeter has clearly the most £100k+ staff per academic. On the fairer FTE basis, no — it is upper-mid-pack (4th of 9), because an unusually large share of its £100k+ staff are part-time (0.63 FTE per head). Both can be true at once: Exeter really does have a lot of people in the £100k+ band, but measured by the work those people do (FTE) it is closer to the pack. That part-time concentration is itself why the 0.8 FTE lever tab is well-suited to Exeter.

Sources, method & caveats
• Source: University of Exeter Annual Reports and Financial Statements 2020/21–2024/25 (Note 7, "Higher paid staff"), archived in sources/. Each report also restates the prior year; restated and originally-reported figures agree in every overlapping year, so the series is internally consistent.
• Figures are basic pay excluding employer pension (except salary-sacrifice), excluding the Vice-Chancellor, and — per the OfS Accounts Direction — excluding staff who joined or left part-way through the year (stated from 2022/23; earlier reports use the same note format).
• The award-adjusted line uprates the £100,000 threshold by the national (JNCHES) pay award as applied at £100k-level spine points: 2020/21 0%, 2021/22 +1.5%, 2022/23 +3% (Aug 2022) +2% (Feb 2023 interim) — Exeter's own 2022/23 report attributes that year's jump to these two increases — 2023/24 +3% (remainder of the 5% 2023 award), 2024/25 +2.5% (verified against published spine values). Cumulative: £100,000 in 2019/20 ≈ £112,600 in 2024/25. Counts above mid-band thresholds are interpolated linearly within the £5k band.
• Headcount, not FTE, in the charts (FTE shown in the table); part-year staff excluded per the accounts direction.
• The professorial-ladder explanation for the £100–110k bulge is corroborated on the Professorial tab: Exeter's ladder tops out at £105,062–£111,459, exactly where the 161-person 2024/25 bulge sits.
