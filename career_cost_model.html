Career & cost model — driven by promotion pace

Earlier basis — smaller population than the Overview. This page prices HESA full-time academic staff only (1,155 in the non-professorial grades). The Overview and the Where the saving comes from tab have since moved to Exeter’s own 2023 distribution across the pay spine (1,934 staff on grades F/G/H, discounted for grant-funded posts), which also counts the professional-services and part-time staff sitting on the very same grades — a grade-structure change applies to them too. The structural comparison here is unaffected: the same scales, the same method, the same ranking. But every total on this page is scaled to the smaller population, so the pounds and post counts are roughly half those quoted on the Overview. Treat them as a lower bound.

Unlike the first model (which assumes where staff sit), this one derives it: staff are appointed at the bottom of each grade, gain one increment a year, and are promoted after a set number of years. Crucially, that interacts with each university's grade length — short grades make staff hit their ceiling sooner. A representative permanent career starts at age 28 (Lecturer) and, on the middle pace, reaches Professor at 48, matching the sector average. Professorial pay itself is excluded.

Assumptions & limits
• Promotion pace sets years-in-grade; the within-grade spread is derived from that vs each university's grade length (staff climb one point/yr, then sit at the ceiling until promoted).
• Salaries above spine point 51 (local non-professorial spine extensions, which differ by institution) use each institution's own published values.
• Career start age 28; one representative path; everyone promotes on schedule — real workforces have non-promotion and attrition, so the trajectory is illustrative; the cost tab instead weights by Exeter's actual HESA grade mix (E&R; SL/AP split 50/50).
• Contribution points modelled as a share of ceiling-stuck staff holding one permanent point; they are the single softest input (no sector data) — refine when Exeter's own data is available.
• Salaries = verified 2026-27 national spine (2025-26 spine plus the 2% award of 1 Aug 2026). On-costs: NI 15% above £5,000, USS 14.5%, levy 0.5%. Excludes professorial pay, London weighting (none in the included universities), transition costs.
• Sector evidence puts L→SL at ~5–7 yrs and Prof at ~48; upper transitions are weakly evidenced.
• Real terms. The negotiated JNCHES award has generally trailed inflation, so the same spine point buys less each year and a career with few increments can lose ground even while the cash figure rises. Setting “Show pay in” to real terms restates every year in £ of the year the career starts, compounding the chosen gap. The measured options compare each award with the CPIH 12-month rate to the August it took effect (sources/pay_awards.csv). Over five years (2021-22 to 2025-26): +1.5% vs 3.0%, +3% and +2% (≈5.06%) vs 8.6%, +3% vs 6.3%, +2.5% vs 3.1%, +1.4% vs 2.8% — compounding to −1.96%/yr, or −9.42%. Over ten years (2016-17 to 2025-26) it is −1.14%/yr, or −10.83%. The two windows are the useful comparison. The decade lost 10.83% but 9.42 points of that fell in the last five years: 2015-16 to 2019-20 were roughly flat in real terms, and the damage is concentrated in 2022-23 (−3.26%) and 2023-24 (−3.10%). Read the 10-year figure as a conservative floor — it is reasonable to assume at least about 1% a year of erosion — and the 5-year figure as the better description of the current regime. Three limits. Those awards are the ones applied at £100k-level spine points, and several settlements paid more at the bottom of the spine — so both figures overstate the erosion for Lecturer-level pay; dial a lower rate on the slider to allow for that. Awards before 2020-21 are taken from a third party's tracking of a single spine point and are not independently verified, so the 10-year figure is the softer of the two. And both are backwards-looking: applying either forwards assumes the relationship continues, which is an assumption, not a forecast. The slider spans 0–2%/yr deliberately: the five-year figure is inflated by the 2022–23 spike, so a forward-looking mean is more likely to sit below it — nearer the ten-year rate — and the slider lets you say by how much. CPIH is used because it is the ONS's designated National Statistic and lead measure. The cost tab is unaffected, because its per-head figures average across career stages rather than following one person through time.
